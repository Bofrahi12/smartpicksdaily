Which app permissions should you turn off? The allow-or-deny guide

Smart Picks Daily · Apps Guides · For adults 18+ in the US

Asking which app permissions should I turn off is the right question, and the answer is simpler than the settings screen makes it look: turn off every permission the app does not need to do its job. Permissions are not decorations — each one is access to something personal: your location, your microphone, your photos, your contacts. An app that asks for more than its purpose needs is either sloppy or hungry for data, and either way the safe move is to deny first and allow later, only when the app asks for a good reason.

1. The one rule: permission must match purpose

Every permission decision comes down to one test: does this app need this access to do what it is for? A maps app needs location; a notes app does not. A camera app needs the camera; a calculator does not. A messaging app needs contacts to find your friends; a flashlight does not need anything at all. When a permission fails the test, deny it — the app will still work for its real job, and you can always allow it later if a feature genuinely needs it. Most permission problems come from tapping “allow” during setup without reading, when the app asks for everything at once. Slow down at that screen: it is the one moment where a single tap decides what the app can see for months.

2. Location, microphone and camera

These three are the most sensitive, so they get the strictest rule. Location: allow “while using the app” for maps, rides and delivery — never “always” unless the app genuinely needs background location, which most do not. A weather app works fine with approximate location, and a shopping app does not need your location at all. Microphone: allow it only for calls, voice notes and recording apps, and deny it everywhere else — a game or a rewards app has no reason to listen. Camera: allow it for camera, scanning and video-call apps, and deny it for everything else. If an app asks for the camera to “verify your identity” before you have used it at all, treat that as a warning and check the app’s reputation before continuing.

3. Contacts, photos and files

Contacts is the permission most apps ask for and least need: only messaging, calling and email apps have a real reason to read your address book. A game, a survey app or a shopping app asking for contacts is collecting your friends’ details, not serving you — deny it. Photos: prefer “selected photos only” over full library access, and give full access only to apps that genuinely manage your pictures. Files and storage: a file manager or a document app needs it; a rewards app does not. Notifications are not a privacy permission, but they are worth auditing too: an app that can notify you can also nag you into opening it, so switch off notifications for anything that does not need to reach you in real time.

4. How to change permissions on your phone

You do not need to reinstall anything to fix permissions — both systems have a central manager. On Android, open Settings, then Privacy, then Permission Manager: pick a permission, see every app that holds it, and switch any of them to “don’t allow” or “ask every time”. You can also long-press any app icon, tap App info, then Permissions, and change that app alone. On iPhone, open Settings, then Privacy & Security: each category — Location Services, Microphone, Camera, Contacts, Photos — lists every app with access, and you can change any of them in place. Do the audit once, then repeat it every few months, or after installing a new app. Ten minutes of switches is the whole job.

5. After the audit: what to watch for

After the audit, watch how apps behave. An app that worked fine before should keep working after you deny an unnecessary permission — if it refuses to open, or demands the permission back with no explanation, that is information about the app, not about your settings. Re-check permissions after every app update, because updates sometimes add new requests. And run the same thinking before you install anything new: our 60-second safety check covers the developer, the Data Safety section and reviews, and our 10-second app check covers the listing. Permissions are the last line of defence on your own phone — and unlike the app store, they are entirely in your hands.

Key takeaways

  • Deny first, allow later: every permission must match what the app is actually for.
  • Location, microphone and camera are the most sensitive — allow “while using” only, never “always” without a real reason.
  • Use Permission Manager (Android) or Privacy & Security (iPhone) to audit every app in one place, and repeat the audit after updates.

Quick questions

Will denying a permission break the app?

Usually not. An app should keep working for its main job after you deny an unnecessary permission. If it refuses to open, that is a warning about the app — consider uninstalling it.

What does “ask every time” do?

It makes the app request the permission each time it wants it, instead of holding it permanently. Use it for permissions you need rarely, like location for a delivery app.

How often should I audit permissions?

Once now, then every few months, and after installing a new app or a major update. Updates sometimes add new permission requests, so the audit is never finished.
