Why your points expire (and the 5-minute fix)

Smart Picks Daily · Points-Expiry Tracker Guide · For adults 18+ in the US

It happens quietly. You open a rewards app you have not touched in a few months, and the balance you remember building is smaller — or simply gone. No fraud, no hack: your points expired, under a rule that was sitting in the terms the whole time. Expiry is the most common way regular users lose value on survey and rewards programs, and it is also one of the easiest losses to prevent. Here is how the rules actually work, and the five-minute routine we recommend to every reader.

The inactivity clock: the rule behind most lost points

The most widespread expiry rule is not a fixed date — it is a countdown. Many programs delete points, or the entire balance, after a set period of account inactivity: 90 days is common, 180 and 365 days appear too. “Activity” usually means earning or redeeming at least once, sometimes merely signing in counts, sometimes it does not. The details differ by provider, which is exactly why people get caught: the rule from the app you know does not transfer to the app you just joined. Two more variations show up regularly. Some programs expire points in the order you earned them, so your oldest points quietly fall off the back of the queue every month. Others expire an entire account — balance included — when the inactivity clock runs out, which is a much harsher version of the same idea.

Program changes and closures

The second path to a zero balance is the program itself changing. Providers rewrite their terms, retire points currencies, or shut down entirely. Reputable programs announce changes by email and give a redemption window — which is one more reason the account email matters (and why we suggest a separate, checked-regularly inbox for offers rather than a throwaway address you never open). If you miss the window, the balance usually goes with it. There is rarely an appeals process afterwards.

The 5-minute fix: a routine, not a rescue

Expired points are almost never recoverable, so the fix is prevention — and it genuinely takes about five minutes a month once it is set up:

  1. List your programs. Write down every app and panel where you hold a balance, with the balance and its cash-out threshold. Most people are surprised by how many they have.
  2. Find each expiry rule. Search each program’s terms or help pages for “expire” and “inactivity.” Note the window next to each program on your list.
  3. Set one monthly reminder. Pick a day. On that day, open each app and do the smallest qualifying activity — one short survey, one daily check-in, or redeem if you are close to the threshold.
  4. Redeem earlier than feels necessary. A balance sitting far above the threshold is a balance exposed to every rule change. Convert points into something real sooner rather than later.
  5. Screenshot your balances monthly. If a balance ever changes in a way the rules do not explain, a dated screenshot is the difference between a support ticket that works and one that goes nowhere.

Consolidate: fewer programs, healthier balances

The readers who lose the most to expiry are usually spread across a dozen programs, a few points in each, none close to a threshold. The tracker routine exposes that instantly. Our advice: keep two or three programs you genuinely use, push those balances over their thresholds, and let the scattered leftovers go. A smaller number of active accounts is easier to keep alive — and expiry clocks only punish accounts you have forgotten.

Quick questions

Can expired points be restored?

Almost never. Some providers restore a balance as a one-time courtesy if you ask quickly, but the terms are on their side. Prevention is the reliable route.

Does signing in count as activity?

Sometimes — and sometimes only earning or redeeming resets the clock. The answer is in each program’s terms, which is why step two of the routine is reading them.

How close to the threshold should I redeem?

As soon as you pass it. Points sitting in an account carry all the expiry risk and none of the value until they are redeemed.
